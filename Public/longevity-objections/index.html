Karl Pfleger’s Answers to Longevity Objections

During the Stockholm Longevity × AI Hackathon (11–13 September 2026), I researched the literature for answers to the most common objections to human longevity.

This webpage is a reading-friendly presentation of the objections section of Karl Pfleger’s preprint Eliminating Aging: Motivations, Objections and Counterarguments, which is otherwise available as a PDF. All credit goes to Karl Pfleger.

01

Objection 01 of 11

Overpopulation

Overpopulation has traditionally been the most common objection to aging research.

Unlikely

Overpopulation is unlikely to be caused primarily by greater longevity. Population depends more on fertility rates than on deaths. Rising fertility rates cause population to grow exponentially, but decreasing death rates only cause it to grow linearly. Fertility rates effectively have higher leverage. Even if adult aging were completely halted, population would only rise modestly: Gavrilov and Gavrilova (2010) calculated Sweden’s population would rise by only 22% over 100 years if all aging stopped at 60. They also show that even the magical elimination of all death would still result in a finite population as long as the fertility rate was below 2.0. For example, a one-child policy with zero deaths would result in total population approaching only double the starting population. Coale (1959) calculated that for fertility rates near the replacement rate, a change in lifespans from 70 years to infinite would only increase population by the same amount as a 10% rise in fertility.

Fertility naturally declines in tandem with economic growth and better healthcare, including lower childhood mortality (Notestein 1945, Caldwell 1982). Globally, the number of children born to the average family has dropped by more than 50% since the 1970s (United Nations 2025). Half of humanity now lives in countries with fertility rates below 2.0 (Gietel-Basten et al. 2022). Thus, the degree to which fertility rates would need to further decline to offset a 3–4x decrease in death rates (from eliminating aging) is well within the range of fertility decline that has already occurred and the expected further decline (Gietel-Basten et al. 2022). Even if fertility rates were not currently expected to decline further, the amount of decline needed to save so many lives clearly represents only a modest adjustment.

Furthermore, not only is overpopulation unlikely to be caused primarily by reduced aging, overpopulation for any reason is increasingly seen as less and less likely. Many people now worry instead about underpopulation or even catastrophic population collapse (Gietel-Basten et al. 2022). Current projections suggest global population will peak this century at roughly ten billion then decline. That underpopulation is increasingly seen as the bigger worry is the most notable change since the majority of prior rebuttals to longevity objections were written. In the past 5 years, India, the most populous country on earth, saw its fertility rate drop below the replacement rate,. and China, which had a one-child policy until 2016, created explicit incentives to have more children—policies many governments around the world are increasingly considering recently.

Not a Problem

Even if population did increase significantly from current levels, people too often exaggerate the problems this would cause. Global population density and absolute population are much lower than they could sustainably be. The carrying capacity of Earth is malleable—innovation can increase it causing less resource use and less environmental impact per person (Cohen 1995 Chap. 11, Ausubel 1996, Goklany and Trewavas 2003).

Global food supply has long been increasing faster than population resulting in higher per capita food availability and this trend continues in recent years (Lam 2023, Sands et al. 2023). Hunger is still an issue in many places but it is primarily an issue of distribution and access rather than total global food production. Prevalence of Undernourishment (PoU) is essentially stable at the bottom of the statistical detection level (<2.5%) in rich countries.

Population growth generally happens slowly enough to allow time for adaptations and innovations. Throughout history there have been Malthusean predictions of catastrophic shortages due to overpopulation that later turned out to be wrong: Malthus’s own circa-1800 famine predictions, running out of whale blubber, 1900s famine predictions, peak oil, etc.. All ended up not being the problems the doomsayers claimed they would (Simon 1996, Tupy and Pooley 2022).

Even without new technology, simple adaptations are possible. For example, water and arable land use are vastly (>20-fold) lower per plant-based calorie consumed vs. per animal-based calorie (Poore and Nemecek 2018) suggesting significantly greater efficiency is possible from simple substitutions, if sufficiently necessary and motivated by good reasons, such as saving millions of lives.

Ethical Perspective

If population growth were the main problem with mitigating aging and the resulting longer lifespans, this is morally equivalent to arguing people who haven’t been conceived yet, whose potential existence is effectively a hypothetical, have more right to life than people already alive. Or equivalently that couples have more right to bring a new baby into the world than an existing person has to not be killed. Either view is morally repugnant.

Cancer Analogy

Few people think society should halt attempts to cure cancer due to population concerns. Nor is there opposition to seeking cures for other deadly diseases. There also aren’t widespread calls for ending the use of existing cures for fatal diseases for population control. Aging should be no different. If cures for each of the age-related fatal diseases are not only okay, but viewed as very desirable, then a cure for all of them must be too. There is no principled line in the sand to distinguish fatal biomedical conditions that society should be in favor of curing versus a different set that it should be against.

Net Good

Even if longevity-caused overpopulation would have some cost to society in increased deaths or suffering, these are outweighed by the deaths and suffering caused by aging. Quantitatively comparing suffering is difficult, but losing one’s home due to climate change is much less bad than losing one’s mind due to dementia. Estimates of the number of people who might die as a result of climate change vary greatly, from 250,000 excess deaths/year from 2030–2050 (World Health Organization 2014) to as many as nine million annually by 2100 under high emissions assumptions (Carleton et al. 2022), but all of these are vastly outweighed by the 36+ million guaranteed annual deaths due to aging if no treatments are developed.

Reversal Test

Imagine an alternate reality in which we all lived on an overpopulated world without aging, in which most in society agreed something must be done to control population levels or lessen the ill effects of overpopulation, but at least no one suffered age-related decline. Now consider the solution of creating a set of molecular processes that slowly tortures every adult starting somewhat after middle age before inevitably eventually killing them, while at the same time creating a lot of demand for skilled care jobs that few people are drawn to and costing millions in healthcare spending plus skilled care. This would be one of the worst possible plans to alleviate the problems of a high population. Better plans are easy to imagine, such as birth control, family planning, or economic development to drive down fertility rates, or more efficient use of resources. Many who consider this problem think involuntary death should be a last resort, but even in that worst case, almost any reasonable way to do it would be vastly more humane than aging.

Longevity Might Help

To the extent to which the danger of overpopulation is related to ensuing environmental problems, longer lives may even help by shifting the average sentiment in favor of better environmental stewardship since people will live longer to see the consequences. Also, better health may itself lower fertility rates.

Objection 02 of 11

Only for the Rich

Many people expect aging therapies to be very expensive and worry it will create a society where the rich live longer than everyone else. This is even a common science fiction plot.

Unlikely

In reality, anti-aging therapies aren’t likely to be expensive for long. Technologies generally get cheaper over time (Nagy et al. 2013). Scale, production efficiency improvements, and innovations improve effectiveness bring prices down. Just as this happens in audio/visual technologies (radio, television, stereos, iPods), automobiles (anti-lock brakes, airbags), and computing (computers, laptops, graphics cards, smartphones), it happens in biotechnology too. All of the above were initially available only to the wealthy and considered luxury items or features but quickly became available to almost everyone, usually within one human generation.

Examples of initially expensive biotechnologies whose costs have decreased include antiretrovirals for HIV/AIDS (Forsythe et al. 2019), insulin (Gotham et al. 2018), and monoclonal antibodies (Chen et al. 2025). Cell and gene therapies are some of the most expensive current treatment types but can and are expected to come down in price in the future (Harrison et al. 2018).

Many therapeutic modalities, such as small molecules, are already inherently inexpensive to mass produce once initially developed. In these cases, scaling to more customers is the best way to recoup development costs.

High costs for modern pharmaceuticals are due largely to needing to recoup high clinical R&D costs, including the costs of failed therapies since it takes roughly ten tries to find a new therapy that gets to approval. Increasing price trends in recent years are largely due to treatments being targeted at rarer diseases with smaller patient populations over which to spread the costs of recouping the R&D. In contrast, everyone ages so the addressable patient population for aging therapies is effectively everyone over 30–40 years old. There are about three to four billion people over the age of 40 globally. Even excluding low-income countries, a profit margin of only $1 could still mean $2+ billion in profits if used by most 40+.

In addition, the portion of costs related to intellectual property (patent royalties) will evaporate when patents expire and generics become available, which happens within a generation. The Inflation Reduction Act in the US enables the imposition of price controls even more quickly, after 9–13 years.

Lastly, there is likely to be a period of time before the arrival of meaningfully effective aging treatments in which their future arrival has become clear to much of the world. Governments can use this time to save and plan for how their use can be scaled to as much of the population as possible as quickly as possible once they arrive (without delaying their arrival).

Not a Problem

Even if aging therapies remain too expensive for most people for a while, governments will be strongly incentivized to subsidize their wider availability since this will save money overall. Governments currently pay astronomical costs for the elderly, for medical care, pensions, and sometimes for subsidized basic care. In the US these are provided by Medicare, Social Security, and Medicaid, respectively. The lifetime Medicare benefits for someone turning 65 in 2025 are expected to be (in 2025 $) $339,000–383,000, rising steadily in future years, e.g. to $586,000–645,000 for someone turning 65 in 2055 (Steuerle and Smith 2025). Add to this $70,100 of government payments per person for long term services and support from age 65 on, for those turning 65 in 2021–2025 (HHS 2022). Even combinations of treatments costing tens to hundreds of thousands of dollars could save governments money if they eliminated most of these outlays, as eliminating aging would.

Aging treatments will also enable people to continue to work undiminished, earning salaries (and paying income tax) instead of collecting pensions. Raising retirement ages is extremely unpopular currently, but this is largely due to people of retirement age already having much less vitality than in young adulthood. Many would keep working if they had young adult levels of health and energy, and others might accept doing so in exchange for access to aging treatments, at least for a time while waiting for treatment price drops. Lifetime Social Security outlays for someone turning 65 in 2025 are $414,000-460,000, rising to $640,000-697,000 for those turning 65 in 2055 (Steuerle and Smith 2025). This represents significant additional potential for governments to subsidize even more expensive aging treatments that would enable longer working lives in exchange for reduced pension payments.

Ethical Perspective

Even while aging therapies are too expensive for most people, it would be immoral to withhold them from those that could afford them. Society did not initially withhold life saving anti-lock brakes from luxury cars and their buyers. Nor was there opposition to initial development on the grounds they would only be available in luxury cars first. Aging therapies are no different.

Analogy to Curing Cancer

Expensive new cancer treatments are hailed as breakthroughs despite their costs. Society doesn’t withhold them from those whose insurance is willing to pay or who can afford them out of pocket, even if others with different circumstances cannot access them. Nor does society discourage their initial development or even the development of more expensive refinements.

Net Good

If aging therapies are so expensive only some can afford them, then the amount of good they provide to humanity in aggregate would be lower than if everyone could get them. If we weigh good versus bad on an individual basis, the balance still comes out in favor of aging therapies for all the reasons addressed earlier. Classically, most wealthy would trade a good amount of their wealth to regain health once it is lost, a sign the benefits of aging therapies outweigh even high costs.

Reversal Test

In a hypothetical world where expensive aging therapies exist and the wealthy don’t age but everyone else does, taking the aging therapies away from the wealthy would be a much worse way to improve the world than working to find cheaper alternatives or bring the costs of the existing treatments down, or otherwise expand access to them via redirecting money from elsewhere such as by government subsidies. Arguably, taking away the therapies would delay how long it takes to improve them to the point where they become more affordable. If luxury car makers had not been able to innovate on improving anti-lock brakes as part of selling them in some vehicles, it would have taken longer before they became available in economy cars.

Objection 03 of 11

Long-Lived Dictators

Unlikely (To Directly Affect Most People)

Worldwide the long-term trend is for fewer dictators and a smaller fraction of global population living under them in closed autocracies. In 1800 almost all of humanity lived in such countries but in recent years this has dropped to roughly a quarter of people, despite a small recent rise of about 5% (Herre 2021). Thus, this is already a decreasing problem.

Not a (or the Main) Problem

Lifespans of the rulers are rarely the main problem in dictatorships. Most of the time, death of the dictator results in continued dictatorship under a descendent (e.g., North Korea) or a hand-picked successor (e.g., Venezuela after Hugo Chávez’s death).

Analogy to Curing Cancer

Many dictators have or will get cancer. Should the world stop pursuing cancer cures and treatments to “take out” the fraction of dictators who will succumb to them? No one seriously advocates for this, or for the analogy for other fatal age-related diseases. Thus, no one should favor the analogy for aging in its entirety.

Reversal Test

Condemning billions of people to suffering and death while waiting for decades is the worst overthrow plan. In ranking ways to overthrow a dictatorship, evaluation criteria should include how many others get hurt (collateral damage) and how quickly positive change happens. In a hypothetical world in which aging didn’t happen but in which we could magically impose it globally, doing so would kill billions of innocents (including those living in dictatorships) and take at minimum the ruler's remaining lifetime. Almost any other plan is better.

Objection 04 of 11

Boredom

Surprisingly, some people seriously suggest boredom as a likely unwanted side effect of longevity so bad it warrants hesitation.

Unlikely

Boredom so bad that suffering age-related decline and then dying are preferred is unlikely: Bored people don’t typically commit suicide to escape boredom despite that it would involve much less suffering than age-related health decline.

Precluding the extension of further healthy life on the presumption one will be bored later is also premature.

Not a Problem

The modern world provides more entertainment over time. There are more entertainment options now than ever before, and more are being created all the time. Games, music, and video & written content are being created faster than anyone can consume even a tiny % of each. Besides those more traditional entertainment modalities, new forms are being created, such as virtual reality.

Social interaction and communication with other people also help avert boredom and methods of communication continue to expand over time, and increase in depth, which promotes closer ties. Communication reach has steadily improved and is now essentially global. Richness has steadily improved from handwritten letters to voice calls to email with attachments to video calls.

Travel possibilities expand over time as travel becomes faster and easier. Previously inaccessible places become reachable. Those who can’t make certain kinds of trips become able to as technology improves. The easily reachable range of an average person has steadily improved over time. Travel is inherently boredom reducing, and in addition it allows in-person interaction with a greater number of people.

Ethical Perspective

Killing the not bored to prevent others' boredom is morally unacceptable. Boredom in one person is not a valid moral reason to force disability or death on anyone else. Even killing the bored themselves would be wrong. Imagine how few children would make it to adulthood if boredom meant being killed. The availability of aging interventions will provide a choice on whether to continue living or not. Anyone persistently too bored to go on can choose to discontinue aging therapies, or even to speed up health decline in a variety of ways (some of which incidentally may reduce boredom).

Reversal Test

In a world without aging in which chronic severe boredom has become a societal problem worthy of concerted efforts to combat it, introducing decades of slow torture followed by certain death seems almost infinitely worse as a boredom mitigation strategy compared to many obvious alternatives: researching the neural and biochemical basis of boredom, testing whether cognitive behavioral therapy can help treat it, subsidizing more entertainmen production, etc.

Often societies erect barriers to risky behaviors (even fun ones) in the name of safety. Some of those increase risks primarily only to the individual doing the activity rather than innocent bystanders. Even if other boredom mitigation strategies failed, removing barriers to fun, risky behaviors, at least for those with diagnosed boredom, is strictly better than slow degeneration followed by certain death, even if those activities have a slight chance of resulting in death.

Longevity Might Help

Young adults as a group are one of the least bored age groups. Much of the extra boredom suffered by retirees and the elderly in general is due to their reduced abilities caused directly by aging. Eliminating aging and the resulting continued good health would allow people to continue working and career building, or changing careers to something new and more interesting to them. Sports, dating, travel, adventure entertainment, and all other entertainment and leisure options would remain undiminished from their ease in young adulthood.

Furthermore, more years of good cognitive and neuromuscular health means more opportunity for education and skill building. Young children are more easily bored than older children with more knowledge and abilities.

Thus, aging therapies might reduce boredom compared to what might be considered normal boredom levels in the elderly today.

Objection 05 of 11

Idea Stagnation and Slower Progress

Unlikely

Human progress is not driven primarily by the inevitability of eventual age-related decline and death. Many economic pressures drive individual incentives that in turn drive overall societal progress irrespective of lifespan: food & survival, competition for mates, the drives for security, property, knowledge, entertainment, admiration, etc. all spur innovation and hard work. Few think the doubling (or more) of lifespans in the last two centuries or so has slowed the rate of progress. In fact, many believe progress is faster now.

Not a Problem

Wasting a resource one has in abundance is a sign of being better off: To the extent to which progress is slowed because people waste more time as a result of having more, this is best seen as a sign of good times. One wastes what one has a lot of. The ability to waste some due to having a lot is much better than having too little—the current situation.

Ethical Perspective

Even if old people’s ideas do stagnate, it would still be immoral to torture or kill them. We don’t currently torture or kill the members of society who produce the fewest new ideas or fail to change their minds. It’s not a valid reason to end lives no matter how many people are involved.

Reversal Test

In a hypothetical world without aging but in which society viewed the rate of new idea generation and progress of civilization as slower than they ought to be, introducing universal slow biological (physical and cognitive) degradation that inevitably results in eventual chronic diseases and death ranks very low on the list of the best ways to spur faster ideas and progress. Scientific study of idea generation and specific training for people on brainstorming, critical evaluation of ideas, entrepreneurship, and the like are just some of the obvious alternatives more worthy of implementation. It’s also possible additional new ideas in the future may come from AI or AI-human collaboration.

Longevity Might Help

The degree to which older people are less flexible or generate fewer ideas is debatable. Even to the extent to which it may happen today, in conjunction with inevitable age-related neurodegeneration, it’s possible this would not be true in the context of maintaining the neuroplasticity and full cognitive abilities of young-adulthood.

The accumulation of more knowledge accumulated over longer timeframes may even spur faster idea generation and progress. For example, some of the biggest explosions in new ideas and revolutions in science are triggered by rapid expansion of work straddling two different scientific fields (Uzzi et al. 2013). Few people generally have time to develop expertise in more than one field, but longer periods of undiminished cognitive abilities would enable many more people to do so.

Objection 06 of 11

Longer Decrepitude

Some surveys that ask people to choose how long they would like to live find most people choose shorter lifespans not longer. People assume longer life means longer spent in a decrepit state of progressive loss of ability and pain. When similar surveys asking how long people want to live specify continued good health, answers flip to the longer or longest responses (Donner et al. 2016). The assumption of a decrepit state has been called the Tithonus error after the mortal given eternal life but not youth by a Greek god (de Grey 2007a, de Grey 2007b, Williams 1999).

Unlikely and Aging Therapies Should Help

The goal of treating aging directly is of course exactly the opposite of this concern. To fix the decrepitude and suffering, not to extend it. In the common vernacular of the field the word healthspan is often described as the goal in order to explicitly disavow the idea of increasing lifespan without extending health. Most interventions specifically targeting aging’s molecular pathologies actually improve health since aging’s pathologies worsen it. The misconception arises when the field is labeled as longevity, rather than aging. Aging is the pathology that cuts short longevity, lifespan and healthspan. The word longevity connotes primarily lifespan for many people.

It is actually the existing traditional healthcare system that causes a lengthening of a predominantly decrepit state, by only attempting to reactively treat late-stage individual-disease-specific pathologies. This is why many in the aging field decry the current system as actually being “sick care” rather than “health care”. This sick-care model’s success over many decades at improving treatments for late-stage individual chronic diseases is why people now live far longer in an elderly state. As a result, most people can easily picture frail and elderly people who are mere shadows of their former selves. Aging’s molecular pathologies degrade health long before clinical stages of age-related diseases, and treating whichever disease crosses the threshold for clinical diagnosis leaves all of the other age-related degradation in place. Thus, it is precisely the current system that already leads to longer decrepitude. Eliminating aging is the solution to this problem rather than its cause.

It would actually be very hard to dramatically extend lifespan without significantly delaying decrepitude since it is aging’s several underlying molecular mechanisms that cause the degradation and subsequently death. After aging processes have progressed unchecked, it’s increasingly difficult to avoid death for longer and longer.

Ethical Perspective

Even if aging therapies did extend decrepitude, it is morally contemptible to condemn the old and frail to death just for being old and frail. This is evident from the fact that trying hard to keep them alive is exactly the system we have now. Not only is there a conspicuous absence of calls for such people to be involuntarily killed, but society expends incredible resources to keep them alive as long as possible, despite their worsened state.

Objection 07 of 11

Unsustainable Pensions or Healthcare Costs

As described earlier, dependency ratios are worsening and the cost of funding pensions and healthcare for the elderly have risen steadily as lifespans have increased. Even without direct and concentrated attempts to extend longevity, many people worry these rising cost trends are unsustainable and governments across the world are heading towards economic disasters. Many people assume direct efforts to extend longevity will make these problems worse.

Unlikely and Aging Therapies Should Help

Just as with the misconception behind the longer decrepitude objection described above, this worry stems from the same mistaken idea of longevity as longer in an unhealthy state. Those too old to be productive or healthy are an economic burden, but longer health is not. Treatments for aging would improve the dependency ratio defined functionally (actual dependency rather than chronological age cutoffs) and reduce this burden. Eliminating aging would optimize the ratio, eliminating this problem.

In contrast, it is the current system of treating late-stage diseases that keeps people alive for increasingly many years without reversing aging’s molecular processes and restoring overall health, and which thereby increases societal costs to care for these elderly, causing imminent dangers of insolvency of healthcare & pensions systems (see Section 2.2). Explicitly treating aging may be the best way to avoid the impending demographic disaster.

With treatments that completely eliminate aging, healthcare costs wouldn’t rise at all above the costs in young adulthood, which are quite low, plus the costs of the aging treatments themselves, which would not increase with passing years (and would likely decrease as discussed in Section 3.2.1).

If people’s capacity to work was undiminished with passing years, it isn’t clear there would be any economic need for pensions at all. In the current system, society in aggregate must pay for roughly 1–3 decades of pensions plus rising government-subsidized healthcare from the output of the productive, healthy 4–5 decades middle of life. In anticipation of the end of working, people often work harder during their working years than they might if they knew they would be able to keep working undiminished in the future. Increasing retirement ages is currently politically unpopular and even with treatments for aging, people who lived their lives in the current system anticipating retirement may dislike the idea of no retirement, but in a world where people enter the workforce expecting to be able to keep working or return to work after an extended sabbatical, saving for retirement may be replaced by a combination of saving for the next sabbatical and/or funding insurance schemes for temporary periods of not working. There would always be an ability to return to work later, whether in the same industry or a new one.

Objection 08 of 11

Unnatural

When people encounter a change they dislike, especially one involving technology, they often disparage it as unnatural. Unnaturalness is not a valid criticism. In fact, the inappropriate favoring of the natural is so common its inappropriate use has a name—the “appeal to nature fallacy”.

Not a Problem

Natural does not mean better: Plenty of natural things can be bad for humanity, such as malaria, cyanide, meteorite strikes, and Alzheimer’s disease. Furthermore, much that is unnatural is good, such as prosthetic limbs, satellite communications, modern plumbing, and pacemakers. One could go so far as to say much of modern civilization: agriculture, antibiotics, HVAC (heating, ventilation, and air conditioning), etc. is essentially a war against more natural states like starvation, infection, exposure, etc.. Thus, being unnatural does not by itself make something worth opposing.

Furthermore, the label unnatural isn’t even as apt as some think. Long lives, negligible aging, and rejuvenation are not themselves inherently unnatural. Some species live for up to 500 years (Nielsen et al. 2016, Butler et al. 2013). Some species exhibit negligible senescence, essentially no aging (Ruby et al. 2018). And at a cellular level at least, rejuvenation to a fully youthful state is a universal aspect of reproduction (Kerepesi et al. 2021).

Analogy to Curing Cancer

Tumors are certainly more natural than radiation therapy, chemotherapy, surgery, or CAR-T therapy, but few claim these therapies should never have been developed due to their unnaturalness.

Objection 09 of 11

Missing Aging’s (or Death’s) Good Aspects

Many people associate aging with several good things, such as wisdom and experience, and worry eliminating aging would rob people of these good things. Some people argue death gives life meaning.

Not a Problem

The view that aging has good aspects is mostly an issue of semantics, especially the overloading of the word aging, or confusion about whether things that correlate with aging are inextricably tied to it. As mentioned in the introduction, the aging we mean here is adult biological aging. Some things that take time naturally correlate with aging, but biological aging is not synonymous with time. The good things that take time (wisdom, experience, knowledge, skill, etc.) do not require aging’s progressive pathological molecular processes for their benefits.

Scientists understand enough about the neurological underpinnings of learning and memory to know they do not depend on the core aspects of cellular aging at the molecular level. Analogously, modern digital learning (AI) systems have demonstrated learning and memory abilities that do not depend on any kind of gradual degradation of their underlying hardware. Thus, avoiding the health decline of biological aging does not prevent the benefits of living, experiencing life, and time advancing.

The idea that death gives life meaning may be related to the fact that rarity often imbues greater value, but meaning is subjective and so vague as to be almost (ironically) meaningless. Do animals’ lives have meaning? There are animals that don’t appear to age and others that live much longer than humans (see Section 3.8.1). Are the lives of such animals consequently less meaningful than other species? Are the lives of small dog breeds that live roughly twice as long as some larger breeds half as meaningful? Few people think we should encourage fatal human diseases in order to instill increased meaning into our lives. Most causes of death are actively fought by society without worries about decreased meaning. Aging should be no different. Eliminating aging would neither eliminate death nor decrease meaning.

Ethical Perspective

Even if aging came with desirable aspects inextricable from its progressive degeneration, suffering, and death, and even if some people preferred this good plus bad bundle, the choice to have both versus neither shouldn’t be forced on everyone. Others who prefer to avoid aging’s ills should have this choice. Similarly, anyone who thinks that death arriving sooner on a more dependable schedule would imbue their life with more meaning in a way they prefer could be allowed to set up their life that way, but should not be allowed to force that choice on others who feel differently.

Longevity Might Help

Quite the contrary to aging therapies robbing us of good things like wisdom, treating aging and consequent increased healthy longevity should actually provide more time for even more experiences and greater wisdom. These are provided by living and learning, not aging. In fact, learning, perspective, and even arguably wisdom increase fastest when young or middle aged. Untreated biological aging of the brain (neurodegeneration) slows the accumulation of these benefits and eventually reverses the process by robbing people of knowledge, wisdom, and skills. Thus it is aging itself that actually robs us of the benefits some fear losing rather than biological aging being in any way a source of more of these good things.

Similarly, many people argue meaning in life derives from the things done with life. Certainly many people who accomplished a lot in their lives are often said to have led more meaningful lives than those who accomplished little or whose lives were cut short before they could do much. Thus, more time could enable more meaningful lives.

Objection 10 of 11

Low Job Turnover (Gerontocracy)

Low Job Turnover Is Not a Problem

Low job turnover is not much of a problem, or at least not made significantly worse by treating aging. Lifetime appointments are probably nonoptimal even without aging therapies. Any specific job should ideally be competitive, and all jobs effectively face competition from other positions or external organizations or countries. Societies can set up unique jobs for which turnover is judged to be critical to have term limits.

Lower job turnover due to longer periods of health and productivity has many positive aspects: To the extent that less aging allows people to stay in jobs longer than they otherwise would, this will allow even greater skill to accumulate. Having more experienced workers not forced by waning abilities to step aside to be replaced by less skilled replacements would be a net benefit to society.

This does not mean new generations will have no jobs. Longer lifespans will allow longer periods of education to allow deeper learning or the learning of things not taught to prior generations. In many areas, chronologically younger job applicants will thus become more qualified for certain jobs. This is especially likely in entirely new industries, which may be coming along at an accelerating rate.

Zero Job Turnover Is Unlikely

There will still be some job turnover even in relatively stable sectors. Some of the older workers will choose to go back to school or change industries, or retire for a time if they have saved enough. Current economic forecasts in many sectors and geographies suggest a lack of sufficient workers due in part to declining fertility rates.

Gerontocracy, a condition in which political power or wealth concentrates in the old, is already a common feature of the world. Currently, the five leaders of arguably the most powerful countries in the world are all in their 70s. Even if better health and less aging enable even longer lifespans, it isn’t clear this problem will become worse than it already is. Also, the phenomenon has limits—voters in democracies often elect young politicians, many celebrities and social influencers are young, and many of the richest people in the world are young and self made. Their wealth did not depend on them aging or an ancestor’s death. The majority of the top richest people in the world became self-made billionaires in their 20s, 30s, or 40s. Even in a world without aging, there is no reason wealth, power, and soft power will not all also be gained by the talented and/or hard working young.

Reversal Test

Forcing suffering and death is the worst policy solution: Even if too-low job turnover were a true societal problem in a world without aging, imposing decades of avoidable suffering and death would be immoral relative to simply mandating job turnover, including for positions of power or influence, via laws/regulations, such as term limits.

Objection 11 of 11

The Future Will Be Worse

Perhaps the most puzzling and vague objection to eliminating aging is the idea the future will be worse than the present.

Unlikely

Despite salient examples of bad things happening in the world, and despite some perceptions of bad trends or the common trope of dystopian science fiction, most hard data suggests the world is improving, not getting worse. By most objective measures the world has been on a clear, positive trajectory, involving less global poverty and hunger, more recreation time, less violence and killing, improving education and health, faster travel and communication, and increasing engineering and computational capabilities (Tupy and Pooley 2022, Pinker 2018, Rosling 2018, Bernstein 2004).

The most common economic measure of quality of life is GDP/person and globally this metric has been steadily improving at a low but fairly stable exponential rate for over 200 years since the start of the industrial revolution (Bernstein 2004).

Ethical Perspective

Everyone should be able to make their own choice: Forcing on some people the choice of not living longer due to other's negative assessments is immoral. Those who expect a better or good enough future should be allowed to embrace it.

Reversal Test

Even if the future is worse, presumably it would have to be much worse than the present for death to be preferable since currently 99.99% of humans choose not to voluntarily end their lives each year (World Health Organization 2025). Even if the future were so bad that death was preferable for a large fraction of humanity, voluntary death without suffering would still be preferable to living in a progressively degenerating state for decades on the way to death.

However bad the future is, there is no option to just keep living only in the present. For however long people keep living, a bad world in which everyone suffers age-related degeneration and chronic diseases would still be worse than an otherwise identically bad world in which they don’t. Thus, in a hypothetical bad future with no aging, creating aging and its ills would not make the bad future better—it would worsen it further.

Source: Pfleger, K. Eliminating Aging: Motivations, Objections and Counterarguments. Preprint, 2026 revision.
